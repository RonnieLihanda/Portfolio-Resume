The problem

Proposal teams lose bids on compliance mistakes, not on weak ideas. A missed form or a broken page limit can get a proposal rejected before anyone reads it. Weekly status reports are the opposite problem: the work is routine, the format is fixed, and writing them by hand eats hours.

Result

From about a week to about two days per RFP response, across more than 30 responses, after moving drafting from manual prompting to Claude skills and agents. These are my own estimates of working time, not timesheet data.

Where the time went before

The proposal process was documented as two procedures. The first covers finding opportunities on government portals and breaking each solicitation into a standard summary: deadlines, scope, scoring criteria, compliance forms, red flags, cost instructions and required attachments. The second covers kickoff, role assignment, drafting the technical and cost sections, sign-off and submission.

AI was already allowed in both, but as loose prompts pasted into a chat, with every result checked by hand against the source. Each response started from a blank page, and the extraction and compliance checking were repeated for every solicitation.

What changed

  • The extraction prompt became a rule set. Every requirement is pulled into a compliance matrix with the original wording, so nothing is paraphrased away.
  • Drafting runs section by section with an approval stop after each one, which keeps a person in control of every claim.
  • Review gates and a submission checklist replaced ad hoc checking at the end.
  • Separate agents for competitor analysis and proposal auditing run the second-opinion work that used to wait for a free colleague.

The RFP operating system

A skill that runs a response from solicitation to final check. It exists in two versions, one for each bidding entity, so one company's qualifications never leak into the other's bid. The rules it enforces:

  • Direction first. Before any analysis it works out the document type (RFP, RFQ, grant) and who is bidding. If a joint pursuit is implied, it stops and asks.
  • Compliance matrix. Every "shall" and "must" statement becomes a row with the requirement quoted word for word. Instruction requirements (format, forms, submission) are tracked apart from evaluation requirements (how the bid is scored), because they fail differently.
  • Amendment and Q&A log. Each amendment and answer is logged, and any matrix row it changes is updated rather than left stale.
  • Go or no-go. A short, evidence-based decision with at most three win themes before any drafting time is spent.
  • No invented facts. Every sentence traces to the solicitation, a company file, a verified fact or a visible placeholder. Missing numbers stay as placeholders and are never estimated.
  • One section at a time. It drafts a section, stops, and waits for approval before the next.
  • Plain human prose. Banned buzzwords, no run of identically shaped sentences, no filler transitions, no vague quantifiers.
  • Final checks. A compliance pass and a quality pass, a scope coverage map, a missing-information list and a submission checklist.
