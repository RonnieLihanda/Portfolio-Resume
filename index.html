Automation

I automate the daily work that keeps a business running.

Onboarding, reporting, team management, opportunity scouting, hiring. If a task repeats every week and depends on someone remembering, I turn it into a system that runs on its own and tells you when it needs a human.

  • Onboarding. Accounts, folders, checklists and welcome messages from one form.
  • Reporting. Weekly activity and hours reports drafted by AI and sent to management, across Jira, Tempo and Confluence.
  • Team management. Reminders that escalate, duplicate checks, manager copies and a log of who was told what.
  • RFP scouting and tracking. A system built on Claude that scouts Sam.gov, FindRFP and other third-party sources for new opportunities, updates Trello, notifies the manager and lists the RFPs that need action.
  • Hiring funnel. A Google Sheets model connected to hiring sites that tracks every candidate through each stage.
  • Meetings. Transcripts turned into decisions, owners and tasks.

Built to last, built to cost less

  • MCPs and APIs. AI that acts inside your tools, and direct integrations where a connector does not exist.
  • Adaptable. Workflows are modular, so changing a tool or a rule does not mean rebuilding.
  • Always on. Monitoring, retries and failure alerts so nothing breaks quietly.

The gap is implementation

Most small firms already try AI. Few get it into the daily running of the business, and the same survey names the reasons: privacy worries, no in-house expertise and difficulty choosing tools. That gap is the work I do. I choose the tools, connect them, test them on your data and train your people to own the result.

How we work together

  1. Pick the one process that hurts most. One, not ten.
  2. Map it with the people who do it. They know where it breaks.
  3. Build the smallest version that runs on real data. You see results while it is still small.
  4. Run it, watch it, hand it over. Alerts, retries and written instructions so it keeps working without me.

Why the stack costs less

I am tool-agnostic and cost-driven. I use self-hosted n8n, Make or Zapier, whichever is cheapest for your volume, plus direct APIs and MCP servers that let AI act inside the tools you already pay for. Every workflow includes monitoring and failure alerts, so it stays available when you are asleep.

Knowledge systems

Helping your team find the right document, and trust it.

Most organisations do not lack knowledge. They lack one place to find it, a rule for naming it and someone who keeps it current. I design that system and get people to use it.

What a good system looks like

One front door

One place to look, with one search box. If staff ask "is it on Drive, email or WhatsApp?", the system has failed.

A simple structure

Folders by function, three levels at most. One naming rule for every file.

Owner and review date

Every document has an owner, a status and a next review date. This is what makes it trustworthy.

Champions, not policing

One person per department, about two hours a month, backed by the COO. Standards are kept by influence.

Built for the field

Works on a phone with weak data. Short pages, checklists, a one-page summary on long documents.

AI on a clean base

An assistant that answers only from approved documents, shows its source and says when it does not know.

Try a sample

Search the sample library below. Each file follows one naming rule, shows its owner area and review date, and overdue files are flagged. This is sample data to show the idea, not a client system.

No match. In a real system this search is logged, because a search with no result is a document that needs writing.

How I would roll it out

  1. Days 1 to 30: listen and audit. Interview each department, find where documents really live and what people ask each other most. Pick one pilot department.
  2. Days 30 to 60: build the minimum. Naming rule, folder structure, document statuses, a one-page champion role and a review cycle.
  3. Days 60 to 90: prove it. Move the pilot onto the system and measure how long five common documents take to find, before and after.
  4. Then scale department by department. Report four numbers each quarter: searches, most-used documents, overdue documents and monthly users.

Onboarding

An onboarding system, written down and mostly automated.

A new starter should not depend on who happens to be free. I write the procedure, assign an owner to every step and automate the repeat work, so each person starts the same way and the manager can see progress.

The written procedure

Every process gets a one-page procedure like this, so it still works when the person who built it is away.

SOP: New starter onboarding

Purpose
Every new starter is productive in their first week and knows who to ask.
Owner
People operations lead. Backup: the line manager.
Starts when
An offer is accepted.
Done when
The 90-day review is signed off and the new hire has given feedback on the process.
Review
Every six months, using new hire feedback.

What the automation does

  1. One form starts it. The manager submits the new starter's name, role and start date.
  2. Accounts and folders are created. Email, chat, tools and the right shared folders for the role.
  3. The checklist is assigned. Each task lands with its owner and a due date in Jira, Trello or Sheets.
  4. Reminders and a tracker. Overdue tasks are chased, and the manager sees one progress view.

I track time to first finished task, check-in completion, and new hire feedback. Targets are agreed with you, not assumed.
